Snapp Market · Order Updates

How a cancellation number turned into a fix

The story of the project, from the first question to the final result.

01

The problem

The company had a significant order-cancellation problem. I was told that approximately 15–20% of orders were being cancelled.

But that number didn't explain why customers were cancelling — or what we should change.

02

Looking behind the number

I took a more investigative approach. Instead of jumping straight into design, I asked to review the most repetitive customer support tickets.

This shifted the focus from a high-level business metric to the actual customer journey.

03

Making sure it was real

A few complaints don't make a trend. So I reviewed the ticket patterns with a data analyst to confirm what I was seeing was real.

04

Finding the root cause

Then I followed the customer journey after checkout. The problem became clear: once an order was paid, it was locked.

If a customer forgot an item, they had only three bad choices — place a second order and pay delivery again, cancel and re-order everything, or simply give up.

05

How big was it?

I ran a survey with 100 users. 81% had forgotten an item at least once. Most of them placed a new order or did nothing at all — the app gave them no better option.

06

What operations could handle

A fix also had to work for the stores. I visited 4 vendors in Tehran: packing an order takes 5–10 minutes, and vendors were already adding forgotten items by hand, for cash.

07

Changing the business team's mind

The business team said no at first. Duplicate orders brought in extra delivery fees, editing sounded too complex, and it didn't feel urgent.

I came back with the evidence: one-item orders waste trips meant for big baskets, and paying twice frustrates customers and erodes trust. And I offered a safe version of the idea:

Let customers add items — not remove them — for the first 15 minutes after ordering.

Fifteen minutes fit inside the packing time. Adding is simple for stores, while removing would mean repacking and refunds. They agreed.

08

Designing it

The order card shows a calm countdown, a clear Add item button and a short note about the 15-minute rule. I put the same card on the Home screen, where people land.

AI suggested adding an upsell popup and a "1 minute left!" alert. I said no — someone fixing a mistake needs calm, not pressure.

09

Planning for what could go wrong

I worked through the edge cases with engineering: if an item is out of stock, the button turns off. If the price changes, the new price is shown. If time runs out, the button becomes Order again. If payment fails, the original order still goes through. And stores can't start packing until the 15 minutes are over.

10

Testing it with people

I tested the design with 10 users. Moving the active order onto the Home screen made a big difference — misclicks dropped from 24% to 6%, and everyone completed the task.

11

Launch and results

We launched it as a 4-week A/B test on about 500,000 real orders. Compared with the old experience:

−38%order cancellations
−58%support inquiries
+22%customer retention
+15%average order value

There was one hiccup: in the first week, people asked how to remove items. A short explanation in the app fixed it by week two.

12

What happened next

The feature ran for about three months, until Iran's internet blackout disrupted operations and cut the longer review short.

Next on the list were removing items with refunds, a longer window for Snapp Pro members, and smart reminders for items people usually buy.

It started with a number. By asking why, it ended with a fix to the real problem — and 38% fewer cancellations.
